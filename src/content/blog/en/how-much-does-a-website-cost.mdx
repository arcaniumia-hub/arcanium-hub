If you have asked three companies what a website costs, you probably got three very different answers. One says $500. Another says $5,000. A third will not give a number until you sit through a sales call.

The truth is that "a website" can mean very different things. This guide breaks down what small business websites actually cost in 2026, what drives the price up or down, and the ongoing costs that often get left out of the quote.

## The short answer

Here are the ranges you will typically see for a small business website in the US. Your quotes may land outside them, but most will fall somewhere in here:

| Option | Typical upfront cost | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DIY website builder | $0 upfront + monthly plan fees | Very tight budgets, side businesses |
| Freelancer using a template | About $500 – $3,000 | Simple brochure sites |
| Small studio, custom design | About $2,500 – $10,000 | Most established local businesses |
| Full-service agency | $10,000 and up | Larger companies with complex needs |

The biggest difference between these options is not the technology. It is **how much of the thinking is done for you**: planning the pages, writing the words, setting up SEO, and making sure the site actually produces calls and leads.

## What actually drives the price

Two websites can look similar and still cost very different amounts. These are the factors that matter most.

### 1. Number of pages

A one-page site is fast to build. A 15-page site with a separate page for every service and every city you serve takes much longer, because each page needs its own content, layout and SEO. More pages is not automatically better, but separate service pages are one of the most reliable ways to show up for more searches.

### 2. Template vs. custom design

A template gives you a pre-made layout with your logo and colors. A custom design is planned around your business, your customers and what makes them pick up the phone. Templates are cheaper. Custom design usually converts better, because every section has a purpose.

### 3. Who writes the words

Copywriting is the most underestimated part of a website. Many projects stall for weeks because the business owner has to write every page themselves. If the price includes professional copywriting, expect it to be higher — and expect the site to launch faster.

### 4. Features and integrations

Online booking, quote calculators, CRM connections, multiple languages and online stores all add time. An e-commerce site is its own category, because every product needs photos, descriptions, shipping rules and tax settings.

### 5. SEO setup

A website that is not set up for search is like a store with no sign. At minimum, your site should have unique page titles and descriptions, a logical heading structure, fast loading, a sitemap submitted to Google, and LocalBusiness schema. Some quotes include this. Many do not.

## The ongoing costs nobody mentions

The upfront price is only part of the story. Every website has running costs:

- **Domain name:** usually around $10 – $25 per year.
- **Hosting:** from free (for simple, static sites) to $30+ per month for managed hosting.
- **Business email:** typically a few dollars per user, per month.
- **Maintenance:** software updates, backups, security monitoring and small edits. You can do this yourself, pay by the hour, or choose a care plan.
- **Paid plugins or apps:** common on WordPress and Shopify. Check the yearly renewal costs before you commit.

Ask every company you talk to: *"What will I pay every month after launch, and what does it include?"* A good answer is specific.

## Red flags when comparing quotes

A low price is not always a good deal. Watch out for:

- **You do not own the site.** Some companies keep ownership, so leaving means starting from zero. Ask who owns the domain, the design and the content.
- **No SEO included.** If the quote does not mention page titles, meta descriptions, Search Console or schema, assume it is not included.
- **Unlimited revisions with no process.** It sounds generous, but it often means there is no plan. A clear number of revision rounds keeps a project on track.
- **Locked into a long hosting contract.** Hosting should be fairly priced and easy to move.
- **No timeline in writing.** "We will get to it" is not a timeline.

## How to choose the right budget

Start with what the website needs to do, not with a number.

**If you are just starting out**, a well-built landing page with a clear offer and a contact form is often enough. You can grow it later.

**If you are an established local business** that wants to show up on Google for several services, plan for a site with a dedicated page for each service. This is where a professional website pays for itself, because each page can rank for its own searches.

**If you serve several cities**, consider city or service-area pages — but only if each one has genuinely useful, unique content. Copy-and-paste city pages do not rank well.

**If you sell products online**, budget for e-commerce from the start. Adding a store later usually costs more than planning for it.
